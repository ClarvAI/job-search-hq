# Interview formats, scoring, and hard questions

Background for `/interview-prep` and `/mock-interview`. Use what fits the user's interview; don't paste all of it into a brief. Sources at the end (checked 2026-10). Laws change: look up the current rule before quoting one to the user.

## 1. What interviewers actually score

- **Most employers that take hiring seriously use structured interviews:** the same planned questions for every candidate, scored against a written rubric with examples of weak, okay and strong answers. In the largest recent re-analysis of hiring research, structured interviews predicted job performance better than any other common tool (about .42, versus .19 for unstructured chats). Practical meaning: the interviewer is filling in a scorecard for a few named skills (usually from the posting), not judging charm.
- **What earns points on a behavioral question:** a real, specific example; what *the user* did (Amazon tells candidates to say "I," not "we"); a result, with a number if there is one; and what they learned or would do differently. Keep the setup short (two sentences of situation, then action and result).
- **Expect follow-up probes.** Government interview guides train interviewers to dig: "What was your role?", "How did you respond?", "What was the outcome?", "Is there anything you would have done differently?" A prepared story needs those answers ready, not just a polished first minute.
- **STAR, CAR, SOAR, PAR:** the same idea with different letters. No evidence favors one; use whichever the user remembers. What matters is the content above.
- **Hypothetical questions ("What would you do if..."):** give the steps in order (understand, check data, involve the right person, act, follow up), then add "I did something similar when..." if a real story fits.

## 2. Formats

**Recruiter phone screen (15–30 min).** A filter, not a deep interview. The recruiter checks: real interest, the basics on the posting (degree, license, years), work authorization, location or onsite days, start date, pay alignment, and whether the user communicates clearly. Prepare: the 60–90 second "walk me through your resume," one sentence on why this role, availability, the salary answer (section 4), and two questions: "What does the rest of the process look like?" and "What's the range budgeted for this role?" (if not posted). Have the posting and the tailored resume open.

**Hiring manager.** Deeper on the work: what they did, how they think, whether they'd be easy to manage. Use the rubric points above and ask about how success is measured.

**Panel.** Several people, often each owning a few skills on the scorecard. Answer the person who asked, but glance at everyone; write down names and titles; a thank-you to each.

**Case or exercise** (consulting, strategy, analytics, product, some operations roles). Either the interviewer leads (you answer one part at a time) or you lead (you choose the order). Either way: repeat the question back, ask one or two clarifying questions, lay out a simple structure out loud, do math out loud, and end with a recommendation and its risks. Stating a hypothesis up front is a popular coaching habit, not a requirement.

**One-way recorded video (HireVue-style).** No human live; usually 3–6 questions, 30 seconds to think, 1–3 minutes to answer. HireVue's own 2025 explainability statement says its scoring uses only a transcript of what the candidate says, not facial expressions, tone or background. So content is what's scored: complete the story, say the result plainly, and use the posting's real terms where they're true. Camera and lighting still matter if a human watches later.

**AI interviewer (live bot by phone, video avatar, or chat).** Now common at the screening stage: in a May 2026 Greenhouse survey (a hiring-software vendor's survey of about 2,950 job seekers in the US, UK, Germany, Australia and Ireland), 63% of the 1,200 US respondents said they'd had one, and 70% said they weren't clearly told up front. How to handle it:
- Treat it as a structured interview: answer each question completely in one turn, with a specific example and a number. There's no small talk to read, so don't wait for encouragement.
- Speak clearly and a little slower; it transcribes you. If a question was unclear, ask it to repeat.
- Don't paste or read AI-written answers. Employers often ban it, it's easy to spot, and it can disqualify the user.
- The user's rights depend on where they live: Illinois requires notice, an explanation of how the AI works, consent, and deletion of the video within 30 days on request; New York City requires notice at least 10 business days before an automated tool is used and lets candidates ask for an alternative process or accommodation (the employer isn't always required to grant the alternative). Anywhere in the US, a candidate with a disability can ask for a reasonable accommodation (for example, a live interviewer or more time).

**Presentation and site or office tour:** see the skill.

## 3. Hard questions (answers come only from the user's real facts)

**"Why were you laid off?"** Short, factual, forward: "[Company] cut [N] roles in [month] during [restructuring]; mine was one of them. I was proud of [one real result]. Since then I've [what they've really done]. I'm looking for [this kind of role] because [real reason]." No apology, no criticism of the old employer. Bring it up briefly before they ask, if it's obvious from the resume.

**"Tell me about this gap."** One or two specific sentences ("I was caring for a family member full-time," "I took time to recover from a health issue, which is resolved") beat a vague "personal reasons." The interviewer's real question is "are you ready now?", so end on readiness and anything done to stay current. Health details are never required.

**"Why the career change?"** Toward something, not away; two transferable skills with proof; what they've done to prepare (only what's real).

**"Why did you leave / were you let go?"** Honest and brief, one sentence on what they learned, then forward. Rehearse it; don't improvise it.

**Questions they shouldn't ask (US).** Employers shouldn't ask about age, marital status, children or family plans, religion, national origin, or disability before an offer (they *can* ask whether the user can do the job's essential tasks, and whether they're legally authorized to work in the country). The user can choose: answer if comfortable; answer the underlying concern ("I can meet the travel and schedule this role needs"); or decline politely ("I'd rather keep the focus on the job; happy to talk about the schedule"). Note it afterward; it's a signal about the employer.

**UK and elsewhere.** In Great Britain the Equality Act 2010 protects age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage and civil partnership, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sex and sexual orientation, and employers generally shouldn't ask about health or disability before a job offer except to arrange adjustments (Acas, "Following discrimination law - Recruitment", https://www.acas.org.uk/recruitment/follow-discrimination-law). Other countries have their own equality laws; look up the job country's rule before telling the user a question was unlawful. The same three ways to respond work everywhere.

## 4. Salary questions

- **Where pay-history questions are banned** (about 20 US states plus DC and many cities as of 2026, and EU countries as they bring in the EU Pay Transparency Directive, which also lets applicants ask for the range before the interview; check the current rule for the job's location), the user never has to share past pay. Elsewhere they still may decline.
- **Order of preference:** if a range is posted, work from it (script in the skill). If not, ask for theirs first: "I'd love to hear the range you've budgeted for this role." If pressed, give a researched **range** whose bottom is at or above their floor, with its source: "Based on [sources] for this role in [city], I'm looking at about $A to $B, depending on the whole package." Never a single number, and never a number from memory.
- **Pay history, if asked where it's allowed:** "I'd rather focus on what this role is worth. Based on market data for it, I'm looking at $A to $B."
- Negotiating happens after an offer, not in the interview (`/offer`).

## 5. Questions to ask (match the person)

The best ones ask what the job really takes. From a long-running hiring-manager advice column (Ask a Manager): "How will you measure the success of the person in this position?" and "What are the biggest challenges someone in this role will face?" Others that work:
- **Recruiter:** next steps and timeline; the budgeted range (if not posted); how many rounds and with whom.
- **Hiring manager:** what success looks like at 6 months and a year; what separated people who did well in this role from the rest; what the team is working on now; how they like to give feedback.
- **Peers:** what a typical week looks like; how work gets handed off; what they wish they'd known in their first month.
- **Senior leaders:** where the group is headed in the next year or two; what would make this team more valuable.
- Skip anything answered on the company's website, and leave pay and time off to the recruiter. Always end with next steps and timeline.
