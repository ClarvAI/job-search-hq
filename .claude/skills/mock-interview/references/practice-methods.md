# How practice actually helps (and how to run it)

For `/mock-interview` debriefs and for coaching between sessions. Keep advice to the user short: one or two of these per debrief, the ones their transcript calls for. Sources at the end (checked 2026-10).

## What the evidence says

- **Coaching and practice raise structured-interview scores.** In a field study of police and fire promotion interviews, attending a coaching session was linked to better interview scores in 3 of 4 jobs, even after accounting for job knowledge and motivation (Maurer et al., 1998). Studies are few and modest; practice helps, it doesn't guarantee.
- **Nerves cost points, a little.** A meta-analysis of 30 studies found that more interview anxiety goes with somewhat lower interviewer ratings (about r = −.19). Practicing under realistic conditions (spoken, timed, with follow-ups) is the best-supported way to make the real thing feel familiar.
- **Interviewers and AI tools score what you say.** Structured interviews use a rubric; HireVue says its AI reads only a transcript. So practice the content out loud, not in your head.
- **Hype to ignore:** exact "% more likely to get an offer if you ask 4 questions" stats, "body-language hacks," and claims that keywords alone beat an AI screen. None trace to solid research.

## Methods that work

1. **Out loud, timed, one question at a time.** That's the Interview Room. Silent rehearsal doesn't train the talking.
2. **Bullets, not scripts.** Each story as 4–5 bullet points (situation in one line, 2–3 actions, the result with a number, what they learned). Memorized scripts break under a follow-up and sound read.
3. **Realistic probes.** Real interviewers dig. Use these when an answer leaves a gap:
   - Vague role: "What did *you* do, specifically?"
   - No result: "How did it turn out? How did you know?"
   - No number: "Roughly how much time or money did that save?"
   - Too smooth: "What would you do differently?" / "What went wrong along the way?"
   - Hypothetical: "Have you actually faced something like that?"
4. **Fix and redo immediately.** After the debrief, re-ask the weakest question while the fix is fresh. A second try with one specific change beats a new question.
5. **Spread it out.** Short sessions on different days (3 questions, 15 minutes) beat one long cram the night before. Suggest a session 2–3 days before and a short warm-up the day before.
6. **Record once.** For video and one-way interviews, one phone recording shows eye line, lighting and filler words better than any description.
7. **Vary the questions.** Mix likely questions with one the user hasn't seen, so they practice adapting a story instead of reciting it.
8. **Nerves:** slow breathing before starting, and saying "I'm excited" rather than "calm down" helped performance in small lab studies (Brooks, 2014). Cheap to try; don't oversell it.
