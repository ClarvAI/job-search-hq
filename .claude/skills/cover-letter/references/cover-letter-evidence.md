# Cover letters: what the evidence says

Background for `/cover-letter`. Researched 2026-10. The surveys below are mostly run by resume-tool vendors and contradict each other; the two economics papers are the strongest evidence but come from a freelance platform, not salaried hiring. Say "evidence suggests", never "recruiters always".

## Are they read?

- **Mixed, and it depends who's reading.** Resume Genius (625 US hiring managers, Pollfish, updated 2026) reported 94% call letters essential to interview decisions and 72% expect one even when the posting says "optional". Jobvite's 2016 recruiter survey found 74% of recruiters didn't consider them important. A widely repeated "44% never read them" figure attributed to ResumeBuilder couldn't be traced to its source; don't quote it. Recruiters on Reddit and in blogs mostly say they look at the letter only when the resume is borderline or already good; hiring managers, smaller employers and writing-heavy roles read them more.
- **When tailored, they help.** ResumeGo's field experiment (7,287 fictitious applications on job boards, 2019-2020): about 10.7% callbacks with no letter, 12.5% with a generic letter, 16.4% with a tailored letter. Vendor-run, pre-ChatGPT, entry-to-mid-level roles; the direction matters more than the exact numbers.
- **Why tailoring is now harder to get credit for:**
  - Galdin & Silbert (2025), Freelancer.com data: before LLMs, employers paid noticeably more for workers whose applications were closely tailored to the job; after ChatGPT, that premium disappeared, because tailored-looking text became cheap for everyone. https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08785
  - Cui, Dias & Ye (2025), the rollout of an AI cover-letter tool on a freelance platform: the tool made letters more similar to the posting and raised callbacks for users, but the link between that similarity and callbacks fell 51% afterward, and employers leaned more on work history. Applicants who edited the AI draft did better than those who didn't. https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25054
  - So: echoing the posting's words no longer stands out. What still can't be faked cheaply is a detail only this user could write about this employer: something they did that matches a named duty, a real link to the product, site, team or customers.

## When the letter is optional

Write one when any of these hold: priority A or B; the posting or a recruiter says letters are read; a small employer; a writing-heavy role; or the resume leaves a question a sentence can answer (career change, relocation, a gap, a later start date, a referral to name). Skip it, or write three sentences, for priority C when there's no real hook: a generic letter adds little (see the ResumeGo numbers) and AI-sounding text can hurt. When the form has a text box instead of an upload, paste a plain version without the letter header.

## Openings and structure

- **Hook first, no throat-clearing.** Open with the role and the most specific true thing: the referral ("Pat Lee on your validation team suggested I apply"), the connection to the product, or the result that best matches the first duty.
- **"Pain letter" (Liz Ryan, 2013):** open with a business problem the employer likely has, then a story showing the user solved one like it. The only evidence is the author's own clients' reports, so treat it as a style, not a proven method. A safe version: name a need the posting states outright (not a guessed weakness of the company), then the user's matching story. Guessing at a company's problems can read as presumptuous, especially from an early-career applicant.
- **Length:** under 300 words is the common recruiter advice and this workspace's rule; three or four short paragraphs. No evidence that longer helps.
- **Country:** UK and Europe often expect a more formal letter; academic letters run longer (1-2 pages). Follow the norm for the posting.
