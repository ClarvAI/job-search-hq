# What recruiters and screening software actually check

Background for `/resume-review`. Evidence first, then what to do with it. Researched 2026-10; re-check before quoting numbers to the user. Say "studies suggest", not "recruiters always".

## The first skim

- **Evidence:** Ladders' eye-tracking study (2018) measured an average of 7.4 seconds on an initial screen, up from 6 seconds in 2012. Resumes with clear section headings, bold job titles, bulleted results and white space did best; multiple columns, clutter and long sentences did worst. ([HR Dive summary](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/))
- **Caveat:** the study published no sample size or role mix, and recruiters disagree on how long they really look ([ERE, 2020](https://www.ere.net/articles/is-the-6-second-resume-scan-a-myth)). In a separate simulation, reviewers spent 2–4 minutes per resume once past the first cut ([ResumeGo, 2018](https://www.resumego.net/research/one-or-two-page-resumes/)). Treat "seconds" as "the first look decides whether they keep reading", not a stopwatch.
- **So check the top third:** name and contact, then (optional) a one-line summary that names the target role, then the most relevant section. A reader who stops there should know what the person does, at what level, and one result.
- **Scan path:** job title, employer and dates on one line each; the first 2–3 words of each bullet carry the most weight. Lead with the result or the strongest verb, not "Responsible for".

## Bullets

- Shape that holds up across sources: action + what they did + result, with a number where one is true. Google's former head of people Laszlo Bock put it as "Accomplished [X] as measured by [Y] by doing [Z]" ([Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.in/googles-former-hr-boss-says-this-is-the-key-to-a-perfect-resume/articleshow/54925993.cms)).
- Not every bullet can have a number. Scale (team size, budget, volume, customers served), frequency, before → after, or who used the result all count. Never invent or "estimate" a figure for the user; ask.
- One to two lines each; 3–5 bullets for a recent or relevant job, 1–2 for old or unrelated ones.
- Duty lists ("Responsible for…", "Assisted with…", "Worked on…") are the most common weak spot. Ask what changed because the user was there.

## Length (follow the target country, then career stage)

- **US/Canada:** one page is the safe default for students, new grads and early career. Two pages is normal with roughly 10+ years or a lot of directly relevant work (common recruiter advice, not a rule). Evidence is mixed: one vendor-run simulation found reviewers preferred two-page resumes at every level ([ResumeGo](https://www.resumego.net/research/one-or-two-page-resumes/)); career centers still advise one page early on. Don't pad to fill a second page.
- **UK and Ireland:** "CV", usually up to two pages, no photo, no date of birth.
- **Germany, Austria, Switzerland:** a tabular CV (Lebenslauf) with exact month/year dates; a photo is still common though not required.
- **Australia and New Zealand:** often two to three pages; longer for senior people.
- **Academic, medical and legal roles** use their own formats (a full academic CV, for example). Look up the norm for the posting instead of forcing one page. US federal resumes on USAJOBS are capped at two pages since 27 Sep 2025 (OPM); see tailor-resume's reference §6.
- These country notes come from career-guide summaries, not studies; confirm with a current local source (a university careers service or the national job board) before advising.

## Screening software (ATS): realities vs myths

- **Myth:** "75% of resumes are rejected by ATS before a human sees them." The figure traces to a 2012 sales pitch by a defunct resume-optimization company, with no published method. In a small 2025 survey (25 US recruiters), 92% said their system doesn't auto-reject on resume content ([Enhancv](https://enhancv.com/blog/does-ats-reject-resumes/)). Small sample, vendor-run; take it as directional.
- **Reality 1, knockout questions:** application-form answers (work authorization, location, licenses, years of experience, willingness to relocate) can screen people out automatically. Harvard Business School's *Hidden Workers* report found many employers' systems filter on rigid criteria, and about half filter out work-history gaps over six months ([HR Dive summary](https://www.hrdive.com/news/report-applicant-tracking-systems-may-exclude-whole-segments-of-candidates/606614/)). So: answer form questions accurately and completely, and give a gap one plain line rather than leaving it unexplained.
- **Reality 2, search and ranking:** recruiters search their system by keyword and job title. The posting's exact terms (where true) and a recognizable title help the resume surface. Stuffing doesn't: a human reads it next.
- **Reality 3, parsing:** tables, text boxes, columns, headers/footers, icons and images can scramble the text a system pulls out, which then fills forms wrongly. Single column, standard headings and a text-based PDF (or .docx if asked) avoid it. Contact details in a Word header/footer are often skipped; keep them in the body. Formats by career stage and when to send .docx: `.claude/skills/tailor-resume/references/tailoring-and-formats.md`.
- **Myth:** "ATS match scores" from online checkers predict interviews. They measure keyword overlap with one posting, not what any employer's system does. Use them, if at all, only as a gap list.
- **Real reason resumes go unseen:** volume. Postings get hundreds of applications and recruiters stop when they have enough good ones, so applying in the first week and getting a referral matter more than formatting tricks.
